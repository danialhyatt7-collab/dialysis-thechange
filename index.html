Case dossier · Preventable death · 27 Dec 2025

It was not bad luck. It was a chain of decisions — and every link had a name.

A patient arrived in a 7/7 emergency and was admitted under a physician who already knew the nephrologist had left the city. What followed was not one mistake but a sequence of avoidable failures. This dossier names where each link broke, asks for the complete CCTV record, and sets out the practices that would make sure it never happens to another family.

Negligence

Four windows where the patient could have been saved — and each was missed

A 7/7 creatinine and potassium reading is a medical emergency: potassium that high can stop the heart within hours. From admission to collapse there were repeated, foreseeable points to act. None were taken in time. Below is the decision trail, then the specific lapses.

17:00 · 26 Dec — Admission without disclosure
A critically ill 7/7 patient is admitted on the promise of urgent dialysis. The admitting physician already knows the nephrologist has left the city — and does not say so, does not arrange a covering specialist, and does not offer transfer.
Overnight — No coverage, no contingency
Through the most dangerous hours the patient has no nephrology cover and no emergency dialysis pathway. Time-critical treatment simply waits for one person to drive back.
09:00 · 27 Dec — Specialist returns, treatment still waits
The nephrologist is back in the building, yet dialysis does not begin. Four more hours pass with the patient still in a 7/7 emergency.
13:00 · 27 Dec — Treatment starts, supervision ends
The nephrologist reviews the file for about a minute and leaves. The technician starts the machine and leaves within ~2 minutes. Visible bleeding goes unrecognised; the patient collapses into hypovolemic shock; the emergency response has no dialysis expertise on hand. The patient does not survive.

i.Treated as routine, not as an emergency

Life-threatening labs demand a rapid, time-stamped pathway to dialysis. Instead the case moved at the pace of one person's travel and availability.

ii.Consent built on a half-truth

The family agreed to admission believing urgent dialysis was available. Withholding that the specialist was away made informed choice — including transfer elsewhere — impossible.

iii.Active treatment left unattended

Dialysis is a high-risk procedure. Leaving the patient within minutes, with no continuous qualified monitoring, removed the one safeguard that catches bleeding early.

iv.Deterioration caught too late

Bleeding and shock are reversible if seen early. They were recognised only after collapse, and the response lacked the dialysis-specific skill the moment demanded.

Practices & SOPs

How to make sure this never happens again — and rebuild trust while doing it

Reform is not punishment; it is protection. Each safeguard below closes one of the gaps above, and each one also strengthens the hospital — fewer adverse events, cleaner audits, and a name patients choose with confidence.

SOP 01 · Access & coverage

Never admit a time-critical case without confirmed cover

Admission for emergency dialysis is conditional on a named, available specialist or an instant transfer plan.

  • 24/7 on-call nephrology roster with a named backup.
  • If no cover, transparent transfer offered before admission.
  • Hard stop: no "we'll manage" admissions for 7/7 emergencies.
SOP 02 · Transparency & consent

Tell families the truth, in writing, up front

Informed consent must include who will treat, when, and what the alternatives are.

  • Written disclosure of specialist availability at admission.
  • Documented option to transfer to an immediately-capable centre.
  • Family receives a signed copy — not a verbal assurance.
SOP 03 · Time-to-treatment

Treat 7/7 as the clock-starts emergency it is

Critical labs trigger a measured, audited pathway — not an open-ended wait.

  • Target door-to-dialysis time for hyperkalaemia, tracked per case.
  • Bridging therapy started immediately while dialysis is arranged.
  • Any delay past target auto-escalates to the medical director.
SOP 04 · Continuous monitoring

No patient is left alone on active dialysis

A qualified person stays at the chair for the full session — this is the safeguard that catches bleeding early.

  • 1:1 or close ratio trained presence throughout the run.
  • Visible-site checks & vitals at fixed intervals, logged.
  • Bleed/disconnect alarms and line checks built into the routine.
SOP 05 · Rapid rescue

An emergency response that actually knows dialysis

The team that arrives must be able to manage the machine, the access, and the shock.

  • Code protocol that summons dialysis-competent staff first.
  • Drilled hypovolemic-shock & bleeding response at the chair.
  • Quarterly simulation; competency signed off and refreshed.
SOP 06 · Evidence & openness

A clear, written CCTV & records policy

Families and regulators get the complete record — by default, not by fight.

  • Published policy: how to request footage, timelines, and format.
  • Automatic legal hold the moment an adverse event is reported.
  • Duty-of-candour disclosure within a fixed number of days.
