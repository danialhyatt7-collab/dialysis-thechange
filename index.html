In loving memory · 27 December 2025

A son lost his father. A family lost an elder, in a place built to heal.

He was a father and an elder, and he came in trusting he would be looked after. He was in the hands of senior, experienced doctors, many of them ex-army physicians who have given their lives to medicine. Yet for a few hours that afternoon, the system around them left no one truly watching. This is not written in anger. It is written so that he is remembered as a person, and so that good doctors are never again failed by a missing safeguard.

Somewhere every day, a son loses a father. A mother loses a son. It happens so often that it stops feeling like news. Wards grow used to it. The danger is not only the loss itself, but how quickly we let it become normal, how easily grief turns into routine and routine turns into silence.

This page refuses that quiet. He was a father, an elder, a whole life loved by the people he left behind. The way out of routine grief is routine care: safety made so ordinary, so habitual, that the next family never has to write a page like this one. Remember the person, and the procedures will follow.
What happened

Four moments when his life could still have been saved

A 7/7 creatinine and potassium reading is a medical emergency. Potassium that high can stop the heart within hours. From the moment he was admitted to the moment he collapsed, there were repeated, foreseeable chances to act, and each one passed. This is the sequence, set down plainly and without anger, so that it is understood and never repeated.

17:00 · 26 Dec · Admitted without being told
A critically ill 7/7 patient is admitted on the promise of urgent dialysis. The admitting physician already knows the nephrologist has left the city, yet does not say so, does not arrange a covering specialist, and does not offer transfer to a centre that could treat him.
Overnight · No cover, no contingency
Through the most dangerous hours he has no nephrology cover and no emergency dialysis pathway. Time-critical treatment simply waits for one person to drive back into the city.
09:00 · 27 Dec · The specialist returns, the wait continues
The nephrologist is back in the building, yet dialysis still does not begin. Four more hours pass while he remains in a 7/7 emergency.
13:00 · 27 Dec · Treatment starts, supervision ends
The nephrologist reviews the file for about a minute and leaves. The technician starts the machine and leaves within roughly two minutes. Visible bleeding goes unnoticed. He collapses into hypovolemic shock, and the emergency response has no dialysis expertise on hand. He does not survive the afternoon.

i.Treated as routine, not as an emergency

Life-threatening labs demand a rapid, time-stamped pathway to dialysis. Instead the case moved at the pace of one person's travel and availability.

ii.Consent built on a half-truth

The family agreed to admission believing urgent dialysis was available. Not being told the specialist was away took away their informed choice, including the choice to seek care elsewhere in time.

iii.Active treatment left unattended

Dialysis is a high-risk procedure. Leaving the patient within minutes, with no continuous qualified monitoring, removed the one safeguard that catches bleeding early.

iv.Deterioration caught too late

Bleeding and shock are reversible if seen early. They were recognised only after collapse, and the response lacked the dialysis-specific skill the moment demanded.

Practices & SOPs

The safeguards that would make sure no other family lives this

The hospital has agreed, in principle, to tighten its procedures and to add new ones where they are most needed. That is the right response, and the family welcomes it. Offered here, in support and not in accusation, are the safeguards that matter most. Each protects the patient first, and protects the hospital and its staff at the same time.

SOP 01 · Access & coverage

Never admit a time-critical case without confirmed cover

Admission for emergency dialysis is conditional on a named, available specialist or an instant transfer plan.

  • 24/7 on-call nephrology roster with a named backup.
  • If no cover, transparent transfer offered before admission.
  • Hard stop: no "we'll manage" admissions for 7/7 emergencies.
SOP 02 · Transparency & consent

Tell families the truth, in writing, up front

Informed consent must include who will treat, when, and what the alternatives are.

  • Written disclosure of specialist availability at admission.
  • Documented option to transfer to an immediately-capable centre.
  • Family receives a signed copy, not only a verbal assurance.
SOP 03 · Time-to-treatment

Treat 7/7 as the clock-starts emergency it is

Critical labs trigger a measured, audited pathway, not an open-ended wait.

  • Target door-to-dialysis time for hyperkalaemia, tracked per case.
  • Bridging therapy started immediately while dialysis is arranged.
  • Any delay past target auto-escalates to the medical director.
SOP 04 · Continuous monitoring

No patient is left alone on active dialysis

A qualified person stays at the chair for the full session. This is the safeguard that catches bleeding early.

  • 1:1 or close ratio trained presence throughout the run.
  • Visible-site checks & vitals at fixed intervals, logged.
  • Bleed/disconnect alarms and line checks built into the routine.
SOP 05 · Rapid rescue

An emergency response that actually knows dialysis

The team that arrives must be able to manage the machine, the access, and the shock.

  • Code protocol that summons dialysis-competent staff first.
  • Drilled hypovolemic-shock & bleeding response at the chair.
  • Quarterly simulation; competency signed off and refreshed.
SOP 06 · Evidence & openness

A clear, written CCTV & records policy

Families receive the complete record by default, with kindness, not after a struggle.

  • A published, plain policy: how to request footage, the timeline, and the format.
  • Footage preserved automatically the moment a serious event is reported.
  • Honest, timely disclosure to the family within a set number of days.
The way forward · a hybrid

The hospital builds the SOPs. An independent eye keeps the balance.

The safest system is neither the hospital marking its own homework, nor rules imposed from far away. It is both, working together. The hospital writes and owns these procedures, while the Punjab Healthcare Commission, or an independent reviewer, checks adherence on a regular cycle. Trust, then verify. That balance protects the patient and the hospital alike, and it is how good intentions become a lasting standard.

"A patient-centric approach" is measured by what is done on the hardest day, not by what is printed in a brochure.

These changes are not a cost. They are the foundation of the hospital's good name. A patient-centric mission is proven by closing the gaps above, and that proof becomes the institution's strongest and most lasting asset.

Solid ground

Documented coverage, consent and monitoring turn "trust us" into a clear, auditable standard of care.

Future trust

Openness with families and regulators shows an institution with nothing to hide and everything to protect.

A good name

Hospitals remembered for how honestly they faced their worst day earn the loyalty no marketing can buy.

A final word

Forgiveness is not ours to give. It rests between you, your Allah, and the deceased.

What the family asks

Written by the family, in his name. Three things, and no more: that he is remembered as a person; that they are given the truth of his last afternoon; and that the safeguards now agreed upon protect every patient who comes after.
